## Scope

B-LINK is a case management system spanning the full public benefits lifecycle for the County of San Diego. Eligibility through data integrations with state systems like CMIPs. Field investigations on mobile apps for BPAI investigators. Adjudication of cross-agency evidence and fraud determinations. Before B-LINK, each stage lived in a separate system at a separate agency. The two-year engagement consolidated all three onto one shared Microsoft Dynamics 365 tenant.

- Parties: County PMO plus three agencies. IHSS (In-Home Supportive Services), BPAI (Bureau of Public Assistance Investigations), and a third adjudication-focused agency. Perspecta as prime contractor, RSM as subcontractor.
- Scale: 2,000 case managers. $600K original contract grew to $2M+ over the engagement. Roughly $150K per year saved on licensing.
- Stack: D365 CE tenant with role-based partitioning, D365 Portals (SAML/SSO) for external users, Azure Functions for state-system integrations like CMIPs, custom Adobe Sign integration for doc-management, Power BI agency-scoped reporting.

## Problem

Four quarters into the engagement, a requirement surfaced no one had scoped. Case managers needed to attach, sign, and route documents through the fraud-adjudication workflow. Sensitive evidence flowing across agency lines, on state-funded timelines that didn't bend.

The SaaS doc-management vendor we'd planned to use (PandaDoc, DocuSign) wasn't on the county's approved-vendor list. We discovered this on a call where, after months of trying to lock down a procurement contact, we finally got the answer: no.

There were a few f-bombs from the product owner on that call. I was in LA, on a remote, watching it happen.

When the dust settled, the move was straightforward: be transparent about what the custom path would require, then bring scoped work back to the table. That worked because of an architectural decision made at kickoff a year earlier. The entire engagement ran on weekly cycles with live customer demos and a shared roadmap. We weren't using weekly demos only to deliver scope on time. We used them to discover scope as we built, and to align expectations as new scope surfaced.

By the time the doc-management surprise hit, the PMO had spent a year watching us surface tradeoffs, name unknowns, and explain hurdles. By then we'd spent twelve months in shared scope-discovery. The procurement block landed as the next item in that conversation, not as a screw-up to forgive. The change order was the artifact of that prior work, not the moment of bravery.

The operator move was engineering transparency from kickoff, knowing scope would move on a two-year build. The customer relationship that survived a doubling change order was the receipt that the upfront design worked.

## Phase 1: Designed for scope discovery (Month 1)

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER — engagement timeline: 2-year horizontal timeline showing Phases 1–5 with month markers and the doc-management surprise point. Optional but high-leverage; orients the reader on the full arc before diving into phase details. */}

Started with entity discovery and mapping documents between county systems and state databases like CMIPs. Built the project plan IHSS-first, knowing the shape would replicate to the other agencies. Locked the weekly sprint and customer demo cadence at kickoff. Not as a process retrofit. A two-year cross-agency build with ambiguous requirements would bury scope surprises under any waterfall plan.

The cost architecture was the other Phase 1 decision. Standard D365 CRM licensing for 2,000 users would have run around $192K per year, well past what the county's budget would absorb. We sat down with Microsoft to model alternatives. The fix was routing the bulk of users (case workers and external-facing program staff) through D365 Portals as SAML/SSO-authenticated external users. Standard CRM seats stayed reserved for admins and adjudicators who needed full access. Total licensing landed around $40K per year. Roughly $150K saved per year, or $300K across the engagement. The Portals decision also simplified the security layer (external users couldn't access CRM data unless explicitly granted), which happened to be the access posture we wanted anyway.

## Phase 2: Built IHSS-first, then replicated the shape (Months 2–8)

Shipped the IHSS instance cleanly: shared D365 CE tenant with role-based partitioning (business units per agency, teams for shared support staff and cross-case investigators), the Portals layer for the external-user majority, and Azure Functions handling the state-system integrations. Once IHSS was working, BPAI and the third agency layered on with the same shape. Less novel architecture, more applied discipline.

## Phase 3: Doc-management requirement surfaces, vendor path blocked (Q4–Q5)

The doc-management requirement came late. Case managers needed to securely accept documents into the CRM, sign them in a way that would invalidate the signature if anyone tampered with the document afterward, store originals in an archaic government document-storage system, and surface the same documents back inside CRM for adjudication review.

We'd been planning on a SaaS e-signature vendor for most of this work. After months of chasing procurement, we got the answer on that LA call: the SaaS path was dead. The only approved vendor was Adobe Sign, which would require significant custom integration to do what the workflow needed.

## Phase 4: Change order signed, custom build shipped (Months 16–18)

Scoped the custom doc-management module against named deliverables — module scope, integration points, security model, workflow features — not labor hours. Itemized deliverables let the PMO defend the spend internally; they were buying a thing, not buying time. Signed at ~$600K of incremental scope, doubling the original contract.

To ship in 2–3 months with five engineers on the local team, I in-sourced engineers from other RSM regions to add capacity without compromising the existing IHSS and BPAI work. The integration itself was nightmare-tier: tamper-invalidating signing flows, secure handoff to the county's document-storage system, and re-surfacing signed documents inside CRM for adjudication — all of it threaded through the Portals authorization layer that made every step harder.

## Phase 5: Staggered rollout and train-the-trainer (Months 18–24)

Onboarded the three agencies in sequence rather than all at once, starting with IHSS where operational confidence was highest. Trained ~20 county leaders — trainers, executives, and program managers — across two on-site sessions and two virtual rounds, shipping documentation and training artifacts that the county's internal trainers used to scale the rollout to the full 2,000 case managers.

## What it proved

- **$600K → $2M+ in signed scope** across the engagement: original $600K + ~$600K doc-management change order + ~$600K migrations support + additional work over the renewal
- **Three agencies on one shared tenant**, a posture the county hadn't done before
- **~$150K/year saved** on D365 licensing via the Portals architecture
- **2,000 case managers** onboarded across agency lines
- **Customer renewed for a support contract** after the original two-year scope completed, citing that the system shipped faster than expected — *despite* the doc-management surprise

Structural insight: customers don't react to scope surprises in isolation. They react relative to the trust capital already built. A doubling change order lands as shared scope discovery after a year of weekly transparency. The same change order lands as "you screwed up" without it. The trust has to already be there. The moment of the surprise is too late to earn it.

## What carries forward

This is the pattern for internal AI rollout. AI work surfaces surprises mid-engagement: compliance edge cases, token cost overruns, vendor approval blockers, model behavior drift. The team's reaction depends on the visibility they had before the surprise hit.

The discipline isn't running an approved-vendor check at the start. It's structuring weekly visibility into the work so when the next surprise hits, it lands as shared discovery instead of a screwup. I run a version of this for my own building: a lessons doc per project, extracted weekly, surfacing what worked and what didn't. When something breaks, the team has already been talking about the surface area.

The CMMC, SOC2, and tier-one-bank-bar conversations have the same shape. The upfront work isn't the documentation. It's the rhythm of conversation that makes a compliance pivot survivable. Naming the constraint also earns the credibility to push back on it next time.

- **Why one shared tenant beat three federated ones.** Three tenants would have meant three security postures, three audit surfaces, three integration projects to stitch the cross-agency adjudication handoff together. Adjudication routinely needed Agency A's evidence alongside Agency B's case context. One tenant with role-based partitioning gave case workers the workflow surface without standing up custom inter-agency middleware.
- **Custom Adobe Sign integration inside D365 CE, not as a sidecar.** The module needed to participate in existing case-record relationships, audit trails, and security roles. A sidecar app would have doubled the compliance surface for no benefit. Building inside D365 CE kept document artifacts inside the same record model case workers were already using. The integration handled tamper-invalidation on signed documents, async handoff to the county's archaic document-storage system, and re-surfacing signed originals back in CRM for adjudication review.
- **D365 Portals SAML/SSO for the external-user majority.** About 2,000 case managers authenticated as external Portals users via the county's existing identity provider — scoped by default, with no CRM data access unless explicitly granted. The scoped-by-default model meant security role design got *less* complex, not more: fewer roles to maintain, fewer places to misconfigure access.
- **Security model: business units + teams.** Each agency was a business unit, isolating case data by default. Teams layered on top for shared support staff (admins, system maintainers) and cross-case investigators who legitimately needed access across business units. Custom security roles defined what each persona could see and do; standard D365 audit logging tracked it all.
- **Azure Functions for state-system integrations.** Eligibility lookups against CMIPs and other state systems ran through event-driven Azure Functions with retry logic and audit-grade logging. A failed eligibility transaction could be re-queued without blocking the case worker's day, and the integration surface was small enough to audit per agency requirements.
- **Data migration with mapping documents + plugin-based tooling.** Owned the mapping documents that translated each agency's legacy data shape into the shared tenant's schema. Used a migration plugin with checksum-validated cutover, then parallel-run testing before each agency went live.
- **Change order structured against named deliverables, not labor hours.** Itemized deliverables (module scope, integration points, security model, workflow features) made the spend defensible to county leadership. They were buying a thing, not buying time. Same structure applied to the migrations and support change orders later.
- **Power BI on top, agency-scoped.** Reporting layer respected the same role partitioning as the tenant. Agency leadership saw their own cases by default; cross-agency views required an explicit role grant.
