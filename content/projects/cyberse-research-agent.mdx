## Business Context

Cyberse helps SMB buyers find the right cybersecurity vendor and makes money by referring qualified leads to distributor partners. I joined as Technical Consultant and shipped an MVP for the marketplace's supply side.

The business needed inbound. The founder wanted to build the agent first and trust the leads would follow, but he was pitching investors on an MVP he couldn't show anyone. 

7 months later the agent was powering 200 vendor pages and a reseller deal with TryHackMe out of a Singapore accelerator. The eval spreadsheet I built for score compression is what got the agent good enough to publish and rank on search.

![The three buyer-facing tools on Cyberse: Peer Benchmark, Explore Solutions, and Compare Products. The agent's scored vendor outputs power Explore Solutions and feed Compare Products.](/images/projects/cyberse-product-tools.png)

## Role

A lot of this was GTM-engineering work: PSEO pages, vendor templates, the lead capture funnel. The rest was prototyping the agent and the eval spreadsheet that ran underneath.

| Artifact | What I delivered |
|---|---|
| Data taxonomy | 12 categories with per-criterion rubrics, calibrated weekly with experts |
| Vendor research agent | Tool-calling against Perplexity, web-grounded scoring per query |
| Eval spreadsheet | Airtable + Google Sheets, cohort A/B tests to refine prompts |
| Vendor pages | Templates for 200+ PSEO pages, agent outputs as page content |
| Founder visibility | Airtable layer over Supabase, weekly demos of outputs |

## Act 1: The Real Bottlenecks

The agent had been in motion for months and stalled. I spent the first weeks figuring out why, and the blocker wasn't the agent itself. The agent lived inside a Supabase table only engineering could read. The founder couldn't see outputs, the design lead couldn't sanity-check rationales, and domain experts couldn't flag bad ratings. It was a black box to everyone except engineering.

So I built an Airtable layer alongside Supabase (framed as a personal tool), and the founder could open it and see every vendor score directly. Within a week he was editing rationales and flagging anomalies himself. Design and domain experts started weighing in too. The project unstalled because more people could touch it.

Once the team was unblocked, I pitched a sequencing change. The founder wanted to build the agent first, then bolt distribution on top. I pitched the opposite: couple them. Every agent run produces a vendor page. Every page that ranks becomes an inbound lead distributors can monetize. **I framed building the agent and building distribution as the same thing.**

## Act 2: Score Compression

The founder flagged that scores felt off. I felt it too, so I built an eval spreadsheet in Airtable and Google Sheets. Each row was a vendor-criterion pair with a 1–5 rating and a short rationale. 

Here's a real cell from the **functionality** row on Drata:

```
5 | "Drata lets teams author or upload policies, keeps version history,
     and links them to framework controls. Over 75 integrations feed
     automated evidence into pre-mapped controls and an auditor-facing
     Audit Hub, reducing manual collection. The risk module scores threats
     in real time, pairs them with controls, and guides treatment plans
     within the same workflow."
```

I was iterating on the prompts and model parameters, but the spotchecks were only helpful until failures became non-obvious. Seeing the score distribution, filtering by category, and reading rationales side-by-side made failures visible.

![The Google Sheets eval spreadsheet. Cohort columns (avg_a / a_5_percent, avg_b / b_5_percent, avg_c / c_5_percent) by category. Pink rows flag failing categories. Green cells flag where retrieval grounding lifted the row past threshold.](/images/projects/cyberse-eval-gsheet.png)

The agent failed to identify 5s and often defaulted to 3-4. I proceeded to run cohort experiments against the same vendor set. 

In the following tests, I'd evaluate whether agents could identify 5s in 10% of a particular category. If so, the agent would "pass" for that category. 

## Act 3: Cohort A/B Tests

**Cohort B: Rubric Granularity** 

- 5 criteria per category, each scored 1 to 5, every rating level with its own anchor description. 
- 25 cells per category, 300 across the set, which I prototyped in Airtable first. 
- Cohort B doubled the pass rate (3 of 12 → 7 of 12). 

Endpoint security, managed security, identity security, and vulnerability management, all categories with deep public material, climbed sharply. The rubric was the bottleneck for those rows.

But GRC platforms came back at 0%, which was a surprise. Drata and Vanta are recent enough that the model didn't have the source material to score them, no matter how tight the rubric got.


**Cohort C: Retrieval Grounding** 

Data security and network security barely budged under B either, for the same reason as GRC: thin training corpora. Categories like Endpoint Security have decades of analyst reports, customer reviews, and vendor docs in every training corpus that exists. GRC as a named SaaS segment is recent.

Cohort C grounded the ratings and rationale using Perplexity. Pass rate climbed to 11 of 12. 

The categories that lifted under C were exactly the ones that didn't lift under B.

## Results

| Result | Why it mattered |
|---|---|
| Scoring pass rate 3/12 → 11/12 | Rubric granularity fixed rubric-limited rows. Retrieval routing fixed coverage-limited rows |
| 200+ vendor and solution pages live | Pages ranked on Google and gave the agent a deployment surface it could be evaluated against |
| TryHackMe reseller partnership | PSEO vendor pages generated the traffic and leads that helped closed this deal post accelerator |

## My Takeaways

- **Not every stuck project is a resourcing problem.** The agent had been stalled for months because only engineering had direct access. Airtable let the founder, design, and domain experts see what the agent was producing, and this unstalled the project. This was more of a team and tooling problem than a technical challenge.
- **When one artifact serves both product and distribution, priorities become clear.** Coupling the agent to PSEO meant every rationale improvement was an SEO improvement, and every page that ranked told us which categories the lead-gen funnel cared about next. If you ever find yourself unsure whether to spend the next sprint on product or distribution, your two halves aren't aligned yet.
- **Understand your evals before you fix them.** I thought something was seriously wrong when the new rubrics and grounding resulted in 40% of Endpoint security solutions scoring 5s. Reading the rationales and speaking with experts helped me see that this was a mature category with commoditized features, and that the 40% was NOT unreasonable.
