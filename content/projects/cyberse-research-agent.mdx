## Business Context

I joined Cyberse in learning mode. The project had been in motion for months and was stalling. The agent's outputs lived in Supabase, engineering-only. The founder was pitching investors on a supply-side MVP he couldn't show anyone.

The business model needed inbound. Cyberse monetizes by referring qualified buyer leads to distributor partners — hundreds to a few thousand dollars per match. No inbound, no referrals, no revenue. The founder's framing: build the agent, the leads come. But the agent was a Supabase table one person could read.

My first move wasn't the agent. It was visibility. I stood up a lightweight Airtable layer alongside Supabase — no migration, framed as a personal tool — and showed the founder he could open it and see every vendor score directly. Within a week he was editing rationales and flagging anomalies himself.

## Phase 1: Resequenced — PSEO as the agent's first deployment (Month 1)

The CEO's original plan was sequential: build the agent, then build distribution on top of it. That path produces an agent with no buyer-intent signal and no leads to put in front of distributor partners. The agent gets technically sharper in a vacuum while the revenue side waits.

I proposed the opposite. Couple them: every agent run produces a vendor page; every page is the distribution surface; every page that ranks generates a buyer-intent lead the distributor side can monetize. PSEO isn't a separate marketing workstream — it's where the agent lives in production. The agent can't drift from the product because it is the product.

The sell to the CEO wasn't that SEO mattered more than the agent. It was: shipping the deployment surface first buys real organic signal, real leads, and real partnership leverage at the same time. The agent gets sharper *because* it's running in production, not in spite of.

## Phase 2: Category-specific criteria, not a global rubric (Months 1–2)

Before writing a single prompt, I sat with the CEO and founding team to map solution categories: SIEM, EDR, ZTNA, IAM, compliance platforms, and more. Buyers compare SIEMs on different axes than they compare IAM platforms or compliance tooling. A global rubric would have made every page read the same — and Google rewards depth and specificity, not generic vendor blurbs. The lead-qualification side was the same problem: distributors only pay for leads with the right profile match, and the profile depends entirely on category.

The unit was a set of criteria *inside* a category, not across the whole landscape. That's what would let pages rank against long-tail buyer queries, and what would let referrals carry enough context to be worth a few thousand dollars.

## Phase 3: Citation-mandatory scoring (Month 2)

Built the agent with tool-calling against Perplexity so every rating cited its source. I chose per-query retrieval over fine-tuning: at roughly $0.005 per query, retrieval was cheaper, and it kept every score auditable.

An ungrounded score is a liability for a lead-gen funnel specifically. If a distributor questions why a vendor got a 4, "the model said so" is a dead end. A citation lets you trace a wrong score back to a misread source and fix it. The retrieval surface and the eval surface had to be the same surface from day one.

## Phase 4: The eval harness — rating + rationale, per vendor × criterion (Month 3)

The CEO flagged that scores felt off. I felt it too. Built a lightweight eval harness in Airtable and Google Sheets — same pattern as the Phase 0 visibility layer, no migration, anyone could open it.

The atomic unit was a rating plus a rationale, one cell per vendor × category-criterion. A typical cell looked like `4 | "Strong native log-source coverage for cloud workloads. Weaker on legacy on-prem integrations based on customer case studies."` — a number anchored by two sentences a human could sanity-check against the citation.

That shape let me do what the Supabase setup couldn't. Pivot by category and see the distribution. Filter by criterion and read the rationales side-by-side. Sort by score and read the model's own hedging language where it appeared. The harness wasn't a dashboard; it was a reading surface. The founder could open it. I could open it. The diagnosis came from reading it.

## Phase 5: Two cohorts to separate rubric problems from coverage problems (Month 4)

Pivoting the harness by category made the compression legible inside an afternoon, but legible isn't diagnosed. Rows were bunching at 3s across most categories. Two distinct hypotheses fit: either the rubric was too coarse to separate vendors (rubric-limited), or the model didn't have enough training corpus on those categories to score them confidently (coverage-limited). Same surface symptom, opposite fixes. I ran them as cohorts against the same vendor set.

**Cohort B — rubric granularity.** Tightened to five criteria per category, with anchored examples and descriptions for each 1–5 rating level. A 4 on "log-source coverage" for SIEMs reads as a different concrete bar than a 4 on "policy enforcement granularity" for IAM platforms. Cohort B doubled the pass rate (3/12 categories → 7/12). Endpoint security, managed security, identity security, vulnerability management — all categories with deep public material — climbed sharply. The rubric *was* the bottleneck for those rows.

**Cohort C — retrieval grounding.** The rows that didn't move in B were the tell. Data security, third-party risk, network security — newer or fragmented categories — barely budged on rubric granularity alone, because the model didn't have the source material to use the finer-grained criteria against. SIEM has decades of analyst reports, customer reviews, and vendor documentation in every training corpus that exists. A category like "third-party risk" as a named SaaS segment is recent. The model wasn't being lazy. It was honestly reporting where its training data ran out.

Cohort C routed under-covered categories through Perplexity for real-time context. Pass rate climbed to 11/12. The categories that lifted under C were exactly the ones that didn't lift under B — coverage-limited rows resolved when the retrieval surface filled the gap the training cut couldn't.

The judgment call was walking the CEO through why we weren't normalizing the distribution to begin with. Score compression in under-covered categories is the model telling you exactly where to route more retrieval. Flattening would have destroyed that signal. Routing followed it.

## What it proved

- **200 vendor and solution pages live**, covering long-tail buyer queries the enterprise side couldn't easily reach
- **200 inbound leads** attributed via RB2B from organic traffic, captured as qualified referrals for distributor partners
- **$200K TryHackMe reseller partnership** — closed on the back of the organic supply-side proof
- **Category pass rate climbed from 3/12 to 11/12** across the cohort sweep — rubric granularity fixed the rows the rubric was limiting, retrieval grounding fixed the rows the training corpus was limiting

When the agent's output is the product surface, eval and distribution share one workstream. Every rationale improvement is an SEO improvement. Every page that ranks tells you which categories the lead-gen funnel cares about next. The two halves don't drift because they're the same artifact.

## What carries forward

The transferable instinct is this: when an agent's output is the user-facing surface, the eval surface and the distribution surface are the same artifact. Don't build sophisticated eval infrastructure off to the side of the deployment — make the deployment readable, and read it.

For Quantifind's feedback-loop priorities: the same logic applies to name-screening. Entities with thin training coverage will hedge the same way Cyberse's compliance vendors did. The right response is to route more retrieval at the under-covered slice, not to normalize the signal away. Score compression isn't a bug; it's the model telling you exactly where its corpus runs out.

And the scrappier the eval surface, the more it gets used. A `rating | rationale` cell in Airtable that the founder can read and edit beats a sophisticated eval dashboard nobody opens.
